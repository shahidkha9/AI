**CHAPTER 3**

**RESEARCH METHODOLOGY**

This chapter explains how the study is carried out. It describes the research approach and design, the source of data, the sampling technique, the analytical framework used as the research instrument, and the procedures for collecting and analysing the texts. It ends with the ethical considerations and the steps taken to make the analysis trustworthy.

## 3.1 Research Approach

The study follows a qualitative approach. Its aim is not to measure how often particular words occur, but to interpret how language constructs political actors and events, and why it does so in particular ways. A qualitative approach suits this aim because meaning in news discourse depends on context: the same word can carry different implications depending on who uses it, about whom, and in which situation (Fairclough, 1995). Simple frequency counts are used only where they help to describe the corpus, for example to show how often each actor is quoted; they do not replace close reading.

## 3.2 Research Design

The research design is a comparative Critical Discourse Analysis. CDA is not a single method but a family of approaches that share an interest in the relationship between language, power and ideology (Wodak & Meyer, 2016). Among these approaches, the study adopts Fairclough's (1992) three-dimensional model, which examines every text at three connected levels: the text itself, the discursive practice through which it is produced and consumed, and the wider social practice of which it is a part. The model is suitable for this study because the research questions move in the same direction, from the description of linguistic features (RQ1 and RQ2) to comparison (RQ3) and to the explanation of ideological implications (RQ4).

The design is comparative because it places two groups of texts side by side: news reports from the print edition of *Dawn* and news reports from two digital-native outlets. To make the comparison fair, the texts are matched by topic and date wherever possible, so that differences in language are less likely to result simply from differences in the events being reported.

## 3.3 Population and Source of Data

The population of the study consists of all English-language hard news reports on the 26th Constitutional Amendment that were published between 16 and 28 October 2024 in the print edition of *Dawn* and in Pakistani digital-native news outlets.

Two kinds of source make up the corpus. The first is *Dawn*, a long-established English-language daily newspaper. Only reports that appeared in its print edition are included. On Dawn.com, such reports are identified by the line "Published in Dawn" followed by the date; stories published only on the website are excluded, because including them would blur the distinction between print and digital media on which the comparison depends.

The second source consists of two digital-native outlets, defined as news organisations established online that do not publish a print newspaper (Nicholls et al., 2016). *The Current* was launched in 2019 [VERIFY: Aurora article] and *Voicepk.net* is an online platform that concentrates on human rights and legal affairs [VERIFY: source for description]. Both publish in English and both reported on the amendment during the selected period. *The Friday Times* is held in reserve. It stopped its print edition and now publishes online only, but it began as a print weekly, so it would be used only if one of the two main outlets did not provide enough eligible texts.

## 3.4 Sampling Technique and Corpus

The study uses purposive sampling, in which texts are selected because they meet defined criteria rather than at random. Purposive sampling is appropriate in qualitative discourse analysis, where the aim is to examine information-rich texts in depth rather than to make statistical generalisations.

A text is included if it:

1. is written in English;
2. was published between 16 and 28 October 2024;
3. is a hard news report whose main topic is the 26th Constitutional Amendment or a development directly arising from it;
4. contains at least 300 words;
5. is publicly accessible and has a traceable date, outlet and URL; and
6. in the case of *Dawn*, appeared in the print edition.

A text is excluded if it is an editorial, opinion column, letter, explainer, live blog or video-only item, or if it reproduces agency copy without change.

The corpus consists of 16 news reports, as shown in Table 3.1. The period is divided into four stages of the event, and each stage contributes two *Dawn* reports and two digital-native reports. This structure ensures that both sides are represented across the whole course of the event rather than concentrated on a single day.

The two digital-native outlets differ in focus: *Voicepk.net* concentrates on rights and legal issues, whereas *The Current* covers general news. This difference is acknowledged as a limitation. Any differences found between the digital-native and *Dawn* texts may reflect the editorial orientation of particular outlets as well as the medium in which they publish. The study therefore describes and compares the texts but does not claim that the medium alone causes the differences observed.

## 3.5 Research Instrument: The Analytical Framework

As in many studies of written texts, the research instrument is an analytical framework rather than a questionnaire or interview schedule. The framework operationalises Fairclough's (1992) three dimensions as a set of analytical categories, each linked to one or more research questions (Table 3.2). The textual categories draw on established tools in CDA: nomination and predication (Reisigl & Wodak, 2001), the representation of social actors (van Leeuwen, 2008), transitivity (Halliday & Matthiessen, 2014) and the analysis of newspaper lexis, quotation and presupposition (Richardson, 2007). At the level of social practice, van Dijk's (2006) ideological square is used to test whether the texts show a pattern of positive self-presentation and negative other-presentation.

**Table 3.2**
*Analytical Framework of the Study*

| Dimension (Fairclough, 1992) | Analytical category | Guiding question | RQ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Text | Nomination | How is each actor named (e.g. full title, party label, nickname)? | 1 |
| Text | Predication | What qualities and actions are attributed to each actor? | 1 |
| Text | Transitivity and agency | Who is shown acting, and who is acted upon? Are actors hidden through passive voice or nominalisation? | 1, 2 |
| Text | Lexical choice and evaluation | Which evaluative words describe the amendment and the actors (e.g. *reform*, *bulldozed*)? | 2 |
| Text | Headline structure | What does the headline foreground, and whose perspective does it adopt? | 2 |
| Text | Presupposition and metaphor | What is taken for granted? Which metaphors frame the event? | 2 |
| Discursive practice | Attribution and source selection | Who is quoted, how often, in what order, and with which reporting verbs (*said*, *claimed*, *admitted*)? | 2, 3 |
| Discursive practice | Intertextuality | Which other texts (statements, documents, other media) are drawn upon? | 3 |
| Discursive practice | Production context | How do the routines of a print newspaper and an online-only outlet appear in the texts (e.g. timing, length, updates)? | 3, 4 |
| Social practice | Ideological square | Is there a consistent "us"/"them" pattern in the representation of government, opposition and judiciary? | 4 |
| Social practice | Wider context | How do the patterns relate to the political conflict over the judiciary in Pakistan? | 4 |

Each category is recorded on a coding sheet (Appendix B) with three columns: the textual evidence (a short quotation with its paragraph number), the analytical category, and the researcher's interpretation. Separating evidence from interpretation in this way makes it possible for a reader to check each claim against the original text.

## 3.6 Data Collection Procedure

The data are collected in the following steps:

1. Candidate texts are identified by searching the archives of each outlet for the period 16–28 October 2024.
2. Each candidate is checked against the inclusion and exclusion criteria in Section 3.4. For *Dawn*, the "Published in Dawn" line is confirmed.
3. The full text of each selected report, including its headline, byline and date, is copied without alteration into a separate file and given an identifier (e.g. D01 for the first *Dawn* text, C01 for *The Current*, V01 for *Voicepk.net*).
4. Each page is saved as a PDF and archived through the Internet Archive, so that the text remains available for verification even if the original page changes.
5. The paragraphs of each text are numbered so that quotations can be located precisely.
6. Bibliographic details, the archive link and the date of access are recorded in a corpus log (Appendix A).

## 3.7 Data Analysis Procedure

The analysis proceeds in four stages that follow Fairclough's (1992) model. First, each text is read several times and coded at the textual level using the categories in Table 3.2. Second, the coded features are compared across the texts of each outlet to identify recurring patterns. Third, matched texts from *Dawn* and the digital-native outlets are compared within each stage of the event (E1–E4), and the similarities and differences are summarised in tables. Finally, the patterns are interpreted at the levels of discursive and social practice, with reference to the conditions of news production and the political context of the amendment. Throughout, interpretations are supported by direct textual evidence, and alternative readings are considered where the evidence allows more than one interpretation.

## 3.8 Ethical Considerations

The study does not involve human participants, so informed consent is not required. All texts are publicly available news reports. They are quoted accurately and in context, and every quotation is attributed to its source. The analysis concerns the language of the texts and does not make claims about the personal beliefs or intentions of individual journalists. Because the amendment is politically contested, the researcher aims to apply the same analytical categories to all texts and avoids presenting any political party or institution as right or wrong.

## 3.9 Trustworthiness

Qualitative research is judged by its credibility, dependability, confirmability and transferability rather than by statistical reliability (Lincoln & Guba, 1985) [VERIFY: add Lincoln & Guba to the source register]. **Credibility** is supported by grounding every interpretation in quoted textual evidence. **Dependability** is supported by applying the same coding sheet to all 16 texts and recording each analytical decision. **Confirmability** is supported by providing the full corpus log and archive links in the appendices, so that readers can check the original texts. **Transferability** is supported by describing the political and media context in enough detail for readers to judge whether the findings may apply to other events or outlets.
