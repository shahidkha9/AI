**CHAPTER 1**

**INTRODUCTION**

## 1.1 Background of the Study

News does not simply report political events; it selects, names and arranges them. When a newspaper calls a parliamentary vote a "reform" rather than a "power grab", or quotes a minister before an opposition leader, it offers readers a particular way of understanding what has happened. Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA) treats such choices as significant because language in the news both reflects and shapes relations of power in society (Fairclough, 1995; van Dijk, 1988). For a country such as Pakistan, where the relationship between the government, the opposition and the judiciary is frequently contested, the language through which political events reach the public deserves close attention.

For most of Pakistan's history, English-language political news has been associated with a small number of established daily newspapers. *Dawn*, founded by Muhammad Ali Jinnah before independence [SOURCE TO ADD], is among the oldest and most prominent of them. Its print edition is produced through long-standing editorial routines: reports are written, edited and checked for the next day's paper, and they appear alongside editorials that express the newspaper's institutional position.

Over the past decade this landscape has changed. The spread of mobile internet and social media has created space for news organisations that exist only online (Yusuf, 2013). Such outlets are described in journalism research as "digital-born" or digital-native, because they did not begin as newspapers or broadcasters (Nicholls et al., 2016). In Pakistan, English-language examples include *The Current*, launched in 2019 [VERIFY: Aurora article details], and *Voicepk.net*, a platform focused on human rights. These outlets publish continuously, depend heavily on social media for distribution and often address a younger audience. Research on online journalism suggests that such conditions can affect how news is produced and presented (Deuze, 2003; Karlsson, 2011), although other studies have found that online and print coverage of the same events can be very similar (Ghersetti, 2014) [VERIFY: author name]. Whether digital-native outlets in Pakistan represent politics differently from a legacy newspaper therefore remains an open question.

This study examines that question through one significant political event: the passage of the Constitution (Twenty-sixth Amendment) Act, 2024. The amendment was passed by the Senate on 20 October 2024 with 65 votes in favour and four against, and by the National Assembly in a late-night session that ended in the early hours of 21 October 2024, when the government obtained 225 votes against the 224 required ("26th Constitutional Amendment awaits presidential assent," 2024) [VERIFY: byline and date of the Dawn report]. The President gave assent on the same day. The amendment changed the way the Chief Justice of Pakistan is appointed, giving a special parliamentary committee the power to select the Chief Justice from among the three most senior judges of the Supreme Court; it fixed the Chief Justice's tenure at three years, created constitutional benches in the superior courts and added parliamentarians to the Judicial Commission of Pakistan ("Explainer: What are 'judicial reforms'," 2024). The government presented these changes as judicial reform, while the Pakistan Tehreek-e-Insaf (PTI) opposition refused to support the bill, and international bodies such as the International Commission of Jurists described the amendment as a threat to judicial independence [SOURCE TO ADD: Dawn report on ICJ reaction, dawn.com/news/1866652].

The event is well suited to discourse analysis. It involved clearly identifiable political actors, it took place within a short period, and it was described in sharply different terms by its supporters and opponents. It therefore provides a suitable basis for comparing how a legacy newspaper and digital-native outlets constructed the same political development.

## 1.2 Statement of the Problem

Pakistani research in CDA has examined political language in English-language newspapers in some detail. Studies have analysed headlines in *Dawn*, *The Nation* and *The Express Tribune*, often comparing newspapers with one another or English-language papers with Urdu-language ones (Butool et al., 2021; Sajid et al., 2019). Far less attention has been given to outlets that exist only online. The few studies of digital political news in Pakistan that were identified for this research are small in scale; one, for example, examines four headlines published on Facebook (Shah, 2025). In the literature reviewed for this study, no study was identified that compares digital-native Pakistani news outlets with the print edition of *Dawn* in their coverage of the same political event.

This gap matters because the two kinds of outlet work under different conditions. A print newspaper and an online-only platform differ in their production routines, their audiences and their relationship with readers. If these differences are reflected in the language of political reporting, readers who rely on one kind of outlet may receive a different picture of political events from readers who rely on the other. If they are not, this would suggest that established conventions of political reporting persist across media. Either outcome would add to the understanding of political discourse in Pakistan, but neither can be assumed without systematic analysis.

## 1.3 Research Objectives

The objectives of this study are:

1. To examine how selected digital-native outlets and *Dawn* represent the main political actors involved in the passage of the 26th Constitutional Amendment.
2. To identify the linguistic and discursive strategies used to frame the amendment in headlines and news reports.
3. To compare the similarities and differences between the digital-native and *Dawn* coverage.
4. To interpret the ideological implications of these choices in their institutional and social context.

## 1.4 Research Questions

1. How are the main political actors represented in the selected digital-native and *Dawn* news reports, in terms of naming, description and agency?
2. What linguistic and discursive strategies (lexical choice, headline structure, transitivity, attribution and quotation) are used to frame the 26th Constitutional Amendment in the two sets of texts?
3. What similarities and differences emerge between the digital-native and *Dawn* coverage in evaluation, source selection and framing?
4. What ideological positions, if any, are supported by the textual evidence, and how can they be explained by the conditions in which each type of outlet produces news?

## 1.5 Significance of the Study

The study is significant in three respects. Academically, it extends Pakistani CDA research from print newspapers to digital-native news outlets, an area that has received little systematic attention. It also applies Fairclough's (1992) three-dimensional model to a comparison of two media contexts, which allows the analysis to move from the features of individual texts to the conditions in which those texts are produced.

Practically, the study may be useful to students and teachers of English and media studies. It offers a worked example of how CDA can be applied to contemporary Pakistani news, with clear analytical categories that can be reused in similar projects. For journalists and editors, the findings may draw attention to the ways in which routine choices of wording, quotation and headline structure can shape the presentation of political actors.

Socially, the study is relevant to readers. A growing share of political information in Pakistan reaches the public through online platforms rather than printed newspapers (Yusuf, 2013). Understanding whether these platforms present political events differently can help readers approach news more critically and recognise that every report, whatever its medium, involves choices.

## 1.6 Scope and Delimitations

The study is limited in the following ways:

- **Event:** It examines only the coverage of the 26th Constitutional Amendment.
- **Time period:** It covers news published between 16 and 28 October 2024. This period includes the final negotiations, the votes in both houses of Parliament, the immediate reactions and the appointment of the new Chief Justice.
- **Sources:** The digital side consists of two English-language digital-native outlets, *The Current* and *Voicepk.net*. The legacy side consists of the print edition of *Dawn*. Web-only stories published on Dawn.com are excluded.
- **Genre:** Only hard news reports and their headlines are analysed. Editorials, opinion columns, videos and social media posts are excluded.
- **Sample:** The corpus consists of 16 news reports: eight from *Dawn* and eight from the two digital-native outlets.
- **Language:** Only English-language texts are included. Urdu-language media are outside the scope.
- **Theory:** The analysis is based on Fairclough's (1992) three-dimensional model, supported by van Dijk's (1998, 2006) concept of the ideological square.

Because of these limits, the findings describe the selected texts and cannot be generalised to all Pakistani media or to all political events.

## 1.7 Definition of Key Terms

**Critical Discourse Analysis (CDA):** An approach to the study of language that examines how texts are connected to social relations of power and ideology (Fairclough, 1992).

**Political discourse:** In this study, language used in news reports to describe political actors, institutions and events.

**Digital-native news outlet:** A news organisation that was established online and does not publish a print newspaper (Nicholls et al., 2016). In this study, the term refers to *The Current* and *Voicepk.net*.

**Print edition of *Dawn*:** News reports that appeared in the printed daily newspaper *Dawn*, identified on Dawn.com by the line "Published in Dawn" followed by the date of publication.

**Framing:** The selection of some aspects of a reality and the act of making them more salient in a text, so that a particular interpretation is encouraged (Entman, 1993).

**Ideology:** A shared system of beliefs that shapes how a social group understands the world and represents itself and others (van Dijk, 1998).

**Ideological square:** van Dijk's (2006) description of the tendency to emphasise positive information about "us" and negative information about "them", while playing down negative information about "us" and positive information about "them".

**Nomination and predication:** The ways in which social actors are named (nomination) and the qualities or actions attributed to them (predication) (Reisigl & Wodak, 2001).

## 1.8 Organisation of the Thesis

The thesis is divided into five chapters. Chapter 1 introduces the background, problem, objectives, research questions, significance and scope of the study. Chapter 2 presents the theoretical framework and reviews previous research on news discourse, digital journalism and CDA studies of Pakistani media, ending with the research gap. Chapter 4 analyses the selected texts and discusses the findings in relation to each research question.
