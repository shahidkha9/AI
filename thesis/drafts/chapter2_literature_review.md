**CHAPTER 2**

**LITERATURE REVIEW**

This chapter has two purposes. It first sets out the theoretical framework of the study: Critical Discourse Analysis in general, Fairclough's three-dimensional model in particular, and van Dijk's concept of the ideological square. It then reviews previous research on news discourse, on digital journalism, on CDA studies of Pakistani newspapers and on comparisons between online and print news. The chapter ends by identifying the gap that the present study addresses.

## 2.1 Theoretical Framework

### 2.1.1 Critical Discourse Analysis

Critical Discourse Analysis is an approach to language that treats discourse as a form of social practice. Its starting point is that language does not merely describe the world; it also helps to shape social relations, identities and systems of knowledge (Fairclough, 1992). Texts are therefore never neutral. Every choice of word, grammatical structure or quotation opens some ways of understanding an event and closes others.

The term "critical" does not mean that the analyst simply disapproves of a text. It refers to the aim of making visible the connections between language, power and ideology that readers usually take for granted (Fairclough, 1989). For this reason CDA has been widely applied to political speeches and news media, where the stakes of representation are high. It is not, however, a single method. Wodak and Meyer (2016) [VERIFY: year] describe several distinct approaches, including Fairclough's dialectical-relational approach, van Dijk's socio-cognitive approach and the discourse-historical approach associated with Wodak. These approaches share a concern with power but differ in the tools they use and in the weight they give to context, cognition and history.

CDA has also attracted criticism. Chilton (2004), for example, questions the assumption, common in some CDA work, that political discourse is primarily a means by which elites control language in order to maintain power, and he develops a more cognitively oriented account of political language. Such criticism is a useful caution for the present study: the analysis must show how ideological meanings are realised in particular texts rather than assume them in advance.

### 2.1.2 Fairclough's Three-Dimensional Model

The main framework of this study is the three-dimensional model developed by Norman Fairclough (1989, 1992). The model views every instance of discourse as having three connected dimensions, each associated with a stage of analysis.

The first dimension is the **text**, analysed through *description*. Here the analyst examines vocabulary, grammar, cohesion and text structure: for example, which words are used to describe an actor, whether a clause is active or passive, and how a headline is organised.

The second dimension is **discursive practice**, analysed through *interpretation*. This concerns the processes by which texts are produced, distributed and consumed. A news report, for instance, is shaped by the routines of a newsroom, by the sources journalists rely on and by the expectations of its readers. Fairclough (1992) gives particular attention to *intertextuality*, the way in which texts draw on and transform other texts, such as press releases, parliamentary statements or reports from other media.

The third dimension is **social practice**, analysed through *explanation*. At this level the analyst asks how the discourse relates to wider social structures, relations of power and ideology, and whether it helps to reproduce or challenge them (Fairclough, 1992).

In *Media Discourse*, Fairclough (1995) applies this framework specifically to the media. He argues that media texts should be examined for the representations of the world they construct, the identities they set up for the people involved, and the relations they establish between journalists, sources and audiences. He also stresses that media practices change alongside wider social and cultural change, which makes the framework well suited to comparing a long-established newspaper with newer online outlets.

The model is suitable for the present study for three reasons. First, its three levels correspond to the research questions, which move from the description of linguistic features (RQ1 and RQ2) to comparison and interpretation (RQ3) and explanation (RQ4). Second, the dimension of discursive practice allows the study to consider how the production conditions of a print newspaper and of online-only outlets may be reflected in their texts. Third, the model is flexible enough to incorporate specific analytical tools from other scholars at the textual level.

These tools are drawn from four sources. Reisigl and Wodak (2001) provide the categories of *nomination*, the ways in which social actors are named, and *predication*, the qualities and actions attributed to them. Van Leeuwen (2008) [VERIFY: subtitle] offers a detailed account of how social actors may be included or excluded, activated or passivised, and named individually or by category. Halliday's account of *transitivity* (Halliday & Matthiessen, 2014) [VERIFY: year] makes it possible to ask who is represented as acting and who is acted upon, and how passive constructions and nominalisations can obscure agency. Finally, Richardson (2007) shows how these and related tools, including the analysis of quotation, presupposition and rhetoric, can be applied to newspaper texts.

### 2.1.3 Van Dijk's Ideological Square

At the level of social practice, the study draws on Teun A. van Dijk's work on ideology. Van Dijk (1998) defines ideologies as the shared social beliefs of groups, which shape how members of a group understand the world and represent themselves and others. Because ideologies are social rather than purely individual, they tend to appear in discourse as patterns rather than as isolated choices.

One such pattern is the *ideological square* (van Dijk, 1998, 2006). It describes four complementary moves:

1. emphasising positive information about "us";
2. emphasising negative information about "them";
3. de-emphasising negative information about "us"; and
4. de-emphasising positive information about "them".

These moves can be realised at many levels of a text: in the choice of topics, in headlines, in lexical evaluation, in the selection of quoted sources and in the use of active or passive constructions.

Van Dijk's earlier work on the structure of news (van Dijk, 1988) [VERIFY: publisher] is also relevant. He shows that the headline and lead of a news report express its most important topics, so that readers who see only the headline still receive the newspaper's definition of the situation. This is especially relevant to online news, where headlines are often shared on social media without the full report.

In this study the ideological square is used as a **supporting concept**, not as a second framework. It provides a systematic way of testing, at Fairclough's third level, whether the representation of the government, the opposition and the judiciary follows a consistent "us" and "them" pattern. The study does not assume that such a pattern exists; it treats this as a question to be answered by the evidence.

## 2.2 Review of Previous Research

### 2.2.1 News Discourse and Political Representation

A central idea in the study of news language is that news is a representation of events rather than a transparent window onto them. Fowler (1991) argues that the language of the press constructs reality through systematic choices of vocabulary and grammar, and he shows how features such as transitivity and nominalisation can shift responsibility for actions towards or away from particular participants. Van Dijk (1988) [VERIFY: publisher] similarly treats news as a type of discourse with its own conventional structure, in which the selection and ordering of information reflect the values and routines of journalism.

Framing research offers a complementary perspective from communication studies. Entman (1993) defines framing as the selection of some aspects of a perceived reality and the act of making them more salient in a text, in a way that promotes a particular problem definition, causal interpretation, moral evaluation or recommended solution. Although framing research is usually associated with content analysis rather than CDA, the concept is useful here because it draws attention to what a report highlights and what it leaves in the background.

Richardson (2007) brings these perspectives together in an approach designed specifically for newspapers. He argues that the analysis of newspaper language must consider not only the text but also the social practices of journalism through which it is produced and read. This position supports the decision of the present study to use Fairclough's model, which explicitly includes production and consumption.

Taken together, these works establish two points that guide the analysis. First, political meaning in news is created through small and often routine linguistic choices. Second, these choices must be interpreted in relation to the institutional context of the outlet that makes them.

### 2.2.2 Digital Journalism and Online Political News

Research on online journalism has asked whether the internet changes the nature of news. Deuze (2003) proposes a typology of online journalism that ranges from mainstream news sites, whose content is largely editorial, to sites built around public participation and discussion. He argues that online characteristics such as hypertextuality, interactivity and multimediality have consequences for the kind of journalism produced and for journalists' professional roles [ABSTRACT ONLY].

Karlsson (2011) focuses on the *immediacy* of online news. Because online stories can be published and updated continuously, the process of news-making, including early, provisional versions of a story, becomes more visible to readers. He connects this visibility to a growing emphasis on transparency in journalism [ABSTRACT ONLY].

Other research suggests that the promises of online journalism have not always been realised. Quandt's (2008) comparative content analysis of 1,603 articles from ten online news media in five countries found limited use of multimedia and interactive features, a fairly standardised repertoire of article types, frequently missing source and author attributions, and a focus on domestic political news [ABSTRACT ONLY] [VERIFY: DOI]. The study of digital-born outlets by Nicholls et al. (2016) reached a related conclusion from a different angle. Examining twelve digital-born news organisations in France, Germany, Spain and the United Kingdom, they found that these outlets were rooted in journalism and that their funding, distribution and editorial strategies were not markedly different from the digital strategies of legacy organisations [REPORT SUMMARY ONLY].

In Pakistan, the shift towards digital media has been documented since at least the early 2010s (Yusuf, 2013) [CHECK: specific points from the report]. More recent research has begun to examine how digital tools affect political reporting; Adnan (2025) [VERIFY: volume/issue], for example, uses a survey of journalists and interviews with political reporters to investigate the influence of mobile media on political journalism in Pakistan [ABSTRACT ONLY]. Such studies, however, focus on journalistic practice rather than on the language of the news itself.

### 2.2.3 Critical Discourse Analysis of Pakistani News Media

CDA has become a well-established approach in Pakistani studies of English-language newspapers, particularly in the analysis of political headlines. Sajid et al. (2019) [VERIFY: page range] examined political headlines published in 2014 in two English-language dailies, *Dawn* and *The Nation*, using a modified version of van Dijk's framework [ABSTRACT ONLY — add findings after reading]. Butool et al. (2021) analysed 25 headlines about the Pakistan Democratic Movement, drawing on the ideological square. They reported that the same political issue was framed differently across newspapers: according to the study, *Dawn* presented the views of both the government and the opposition, whereas *Express* foregrounded the government's unity and strength. The authors concluded that newspaper headlines reflect the leanings of their editors [ABSTRACT ONLY].

A related line of research compares English-language and Urdu-language newspapers. Ahmed and Memon (2024) [VERIFY: page range] analysed front-page headlines in two English and two Urdu newspapers during the opposition's no-confidence motion against the PTI government. They reported a marked difference between the two groups, with Urdu newspapers using more sensational wording, and concluded that the language of the media can be shaped to favour either the government or the opposition [ABSTRACT ONLY].

Studies of digital political news in Pakistan are fewer. Shah (2025) combined Fairclough's three-dimensional model with van Dijk's socio-cognitive approach to analyse political headlines on Pakistani social media. The study reported that interrogative and exclamatory sentences, hyperbole and selective omission were used to create sensationalism, and that visual elements such as images of prominent figures shaped how audiences interpreted the headlines [ABSTRACT ONLY]. Its sample, however, consisted of only four Facebook headlines, which limits how far its conclusions can be generalised.

These studies show that Pakistani CDA research has developed useful tools for analysing political headlines and has repeatedly found that newspapers construct the same political events in different ways. Two limitations stand out. First, most studies concentrate on headlines rather than full news reports. Second, the comparisons they make are usually between newspapers, or between English and Urdu newspapers, rather than between legacy print media and outlets that exist only online.

### 2.2.4 Comparative Studies of Online and Print News

International research that directly compares online and print news has produced mixed results. Ghersetti (2014) [VERIFY: author] compared the online and print coverage of major Swedish newspapers during the 2010 election campaign and found no significant differences in how they reported the aspects, issues and actors of the campaign [ABSTRACT ONLY]. This finding suggests that, at least in established news organisations, online and print journalism may follow the same conventions.

The arguments of Deuze (2003) and Karlsson (2011), by contrast, imply that the conditions of online production, such as speed, continuous updating and new forms of audience interaction, can change the character of news. The two positions are not necessarily contradictory. Ghersetti's study compared the print and online versions of the *same* newspapers, whose online content was produced within the same organisations, whereas digital-native outlets operate without a print tradition at all. Whether digital-native outlets differ more clearly from legacy print newspapers is therefore an open question.

It should also be noted that most of this comparative research uses quantitative content analysis, which measures topics, actors and formats across large samples. Such studies are valuable for identifying broad patterns but are not designed to examine how meaning is constructed through specific linguistic choices. A qualitative CDA comparison can complement them by showing *how* similar or different representations are achieved in language.

## 2.3 Research Gap

The literature reviewed in this chapter points to a clear gap. Pakistani CDA research has examined political language in English-language newspapers such as *Dawn*, *The Nation* and *Express*, and has compared newspapers with one another and English-language papers with Urdu-language ones (Ahmed & Memon, 2024; Butool et al., 2021; Sajid et al., 2019). Research on digital political news in Pakistan, by contrast, remains limited and small in scale (Shah, 2025). Internationally, comparisons between online and print news have mostly relied on quantitative content analysis and have produced mixed findings (Ghersetti, 2014; Quandt, 2008).

In the literature reviewed for this study, no study was identified that uses Critical Discourse Analysis to compare digital-native Pakistani news outlets with the print edition of *Dawn* in their coverage of the same political event. This is a contextual gap, because the Pakistani digital-native news sector has received little discourse-analytic attention, and a methodological gap, because existing comparisons of online and print news rarely examine full news reports qualitatively. The present study addresses both by applying Fairclough's three-dimensional model, supported by van Dijk's ideological square, to matched news reports on the 26th Constitutional Amendment.
