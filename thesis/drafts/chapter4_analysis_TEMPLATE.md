# Writing method for every analytical paragraph

Use this six-step recipe (from the project brief) for each point:

1. **Feature:** name the linguistic feature (e.g. "agentless passive").
2. **Evidence:** give a short exact quotation with its ID and paragraph: (D03, para. 2).
3. **Function:** explain what the feature does linguistically.
4. **Comparison:** set it against the matched text(s) from the other side.
5. **Link:** connect it to the RQ and to Fairclough's level (text / discursive / social).
6. **Alternative reading:** note another plausible interpretation where one exists.

# TRAINING EXAMPLE — practice only, NOT thesis content

**Status of the evidence:** the two headlines below were confirmed as real headlines through web search on 14 October 2026. **The full reports have not been read.** Neither text has yet been checked against the inclusion criteria; the *Dawn* item is labelled "Pakistan" on Dawn.com and may be web-only, which would exclude it. This example exists only to show the method. It must not be copied into Chapter 4 unless both texts enter the corpus and the analysis is re-checked against the full reports.

- *Dawn* (dawn.com/news/1866452): "26th Constitutional Amendment awaits presidential assent after sailing through NA in late-night session"
- *The Current*: "Long-awaited 26th Amendment tabled; PTI skips voting"

**Applying the recipe (headline level only):**

1. **Feature: metaphor and agency.** The *Dawn* headline uses the metaphor *sailing through* for the passage of the bill.
2. **Evidence:** "after sailing through NA in late-night session".
3. **Function:** *Sailing through* suggests ease and lack of obstruction. Grammatically, the amendment itself is the subject; the people who voted for it are not named, so the agency of the government and coalition is backgrounded. The phrase *late-night session* adds a detail about timing without explicitly evaluating it.
4. **Comparison:** *The Current*'s headline makes a political actor, PTI, the grammatical agent of the second clause ("PTI skips voting"). The verb *skips* is informal and may imply avoidance, rather than the more formal *boycotts* or *abstains*. The adjective *Long-awaited* presupposes that the amendment had been expected or delayed.
5. **Link (RQ1, RQ2; textual level):** the two headlines differ in *whose* action is foregrounded: the bill's procedural progress (*Dawn*) versus the opposition's behaviour (*The Current*).
6. **Alternative readings:** *sailing through* could be read as quietly critical (the bill passed too easily) or simply as conventional headline idiom; *skips* may reflect *The Current*'s informal, youth-oriented style rather than an evaluation of PTI. Only the full texts, and patterns across several texts, could decide between these readings.

Notice what this example does **not** do: it does not claim that either outlet is biased. Two headlines are far too little evidence for that.
